A · Squad, promotion & transfer context

Neither club is promoted. Bournemouth finished 6th in 2025–26 on 57 points — 13 wins, 18 draws, 7 defeats, 58 scored, 54 conceded, and qualified for the Europa League; the 18 draws are the number to hold on to, because they are the most of any side in the division and the club has since drawn three of its first four this season. Liverpool finished 5th on 60 points — 17 wins, 9 draws, 12 defeats, 63 scored, 53 conceded, which was enough for the Champions League and not enough for the manager. The structural headline is that this fixture is the transaction itself: Liverpool dismissed Arne Slot and appointed Andoni Iraola, Bournemouth’s head coach, who returns to the Vitality Stadium today in the away dugout — and both clubs then rebuilt at opposite scales. Liverpool spent roughly £247m against £700,000 of income, releasing Mohamed Salah and Ibrahima Konaté and letting Andrew Robertson leave free; Bournemouth spent £54m against nothing, replacing their centre-back, right-back, goalkeeper and centre-forward, and appointed Marco Rose. Every meaningful number on this card belongs to a coach who is no longer at the club that produced it — and on Liverpool’s side, to a forward line that has gone.

B · Style & early-season reality

Bournemouth under Rose look, so far, almost exactly like Bournemouth under Iraola, which is the opposite of what a £54m spine replacement and a new German coach would suggest. PPDA 9.55 against 9.68 last season, possession 47.9% against 50.1%, xG 1.79 against 1.76, xG conceded 1.43 against 1.49 — four of the most style-sensitive series in the dataset, all within noise of the previous regime. Shots are up slightly (14.75 against 13.79) and shots on target sharply (6.00 against 4.66), deep completions are marginally down (6.50 against 7.24). What has changed is the discipline column and it has changed a lot: 3.25 cards per match against 2.37 last season, on almost identical fouls, 12.25 against 12.03 — more bookings from the same number of offences. The result profile is the club’s signature amplified: no clean sheet and no blank in four matches, both teams scoring in 100% of them, and three draws. A side that drew 18 of 38 has started by drawing three of four.

Liverpool are the side that has actually changed shape, and the change is Iraola’s trademark rather than an inference. PPDA has fallen from 10.03 to 7.06 — by a distance the most aggressive press of any club carded on this slate, and a 30% increase in pressing intensity on the same possession share (60.3% against 59.3%). Two things move with it. Fouls have risen from 10.08 to 13.00 per match and cards from 1.53 to 2.50 — Liverpool were the cleanest side in the Premier League last season and are now booking at over one and a half times their own rate, which is what a press that wins the ball higher and more often concedes at the other end of the same action. And deep completions have collapsed from 9.82 to 4.75 per match: they are getting into the final third far less often, while their xG holds at 1.74 against 1.77. That is a side creating the same volume from fewer, faster entries rather than from sustained territory. The attacking personnel behind it is entirely new — Salah released, Barcola at £123m, Muñoz at £34.5m, Isak and Wirtz in the expected eleven — so the xG continuity is being produced by different players in a different structure, which is the reason it should not be read as continuity. The one clear result-level consequence so far: three draws in four, and 1.00 conceded per match against 1.39.

C · Market-type suitability

1X2 / MoneylineDangerous
Both clubs changed head coach and one of the changes is this fixture — Iraola left Bournemouth for Liverpool, so neither club’s 2025–26 record describes the team that will be picked. Liverpool spent £247m and released their record scorer; Bournemouth replaced four of their spine positions. What has emerged is a pile of draws: six from eight league matches between them this season, 21 from the last 42. The division’s home-win rate has fallen to 37.0% from 42.6% with draws at 26.1%. Two unbeaten-or-nearly sides that cannot stop drawing, with new coaches at both ends, is a result nothing on this card speaks to.
Asian handicap / DNBDangerous — and the draw density is the specific reason
A draw-no-bet or handicap line is worst placed exactly where draws dominate, and these two are the densest draw pairing on the slate. Bournemouth have drawn 21 of their last 42 league matches, Liverpool 12 of theirs; six of the eight league matches they have played this season finished level. Bournemouth’s four margins are −1, 0, 0, 0; Liverpool’s are 0, 0, +2, 0. Seven of eight are within one goal. That distribution makes the handicap pricing problem hardest at exactly the line it would be set at, and the DNB variant removes the single most likely outcome from the market.
Totals (O/U goals)Well-suited — both sides’ goal evidence survives the coaching change
This is the rare family where the counted rate and the modelled rate agree across the regime change on both sides. Bournemouth: 55.3% over 2.5 last season and 75.0% this, with xG 1.76 then 1.79 and xG conceded 1.49 then 1.43 — four series essentially unmoved by a new coach and a £54m spine. Liverpool: 60.5% then 50.0%, xG 1.77 then 1.74. Both clubs are above the divisional xG line of 1.63 per team, and Bournemouth’s four matches have produced 13 goals. The division is at 2.83 goals per game against 2.75 — the smallest baseline movement of the five leagues on this slate, so the reference point here is unusually stable. The caveat is Liverpool’s 0–0 with Fulham and a 1.00 goals-conceded rate under a coach pressing 30% harder; that is four matches against Bournemouth’s 42 of consistency.
BTTSWell-suited — the single best-evidenced line on this card
These were the two highest BTTS rates in the Premier League last season and the pattern has not broken. Bournemouth 65.8% and Liverpool 68.4% across 38 matches each, against a divisional 56.1%; Bournemouth 100% across four this season and Liverpool 50%. Underneath it: Bournemouth have not kept a league clean sheet this season and have not failed to score either — 0% and 0% across four matches — and last season both figures were extreme in the same direction (28.9% clean sheets, 18.4% blanks; Liverpool 10.5% blanks, the lowest in the division). Both clubs concede at or above 1.4 xG and create at or above 1.74. The evidence spans two seasons, two coaches per club and 84 matches, which is more than any other family on this card can claim.
Team goalsOK — Bournemouth-side firm, Liverpool-side rebuilt
Bournemouth’s attacking series is the most stable thing in this fixture: 13.79 shots then 14.75, 4.66 on target then 6.00, 1.76 xG then 1.79, 7.24 deep completions then 6.50 — across a change of coach and a new centre-forward at £21.5m. Liverpool’s total is equally stable (1.77 then 1.74 xG, 15.50 then 16.00 shots) but its composition is not: Salah released, Barcola at £123m, Muñoz at £34.5m, Ekitike and Chiesa injured, and deep completions halved from 9.82 to 4.75. The same xG arriving from half the final-third entries with an entirely new front line is a different production process reaching the same number over four matches. The clean-sheet and failed-to-score sub-lines point in opposite directions and both are short: Liverpool have kept clean sheets in 50% of four matches against 26.3% last season, Bournemouth have failed to score in 0% of four against 18.4%, and one of those has to give.

Bournemouth’s first-half attacking rate has nearly doubled and it is not one match: 1.25 scored before the break this season against 0.68 last, having led at half-time in three of their four league matches (1–0 at City, 1–0 v Everton, 2–1 at Newcastle, 1–1 v Brentford). That is a counted change with a plausible structural companion in Rose’s higher shots-on-target rate. Against it: every one of those leads was surrendered or reduced, which is the draw pattern showing up inside the match as well as at the end of it. Liverpool are flat at 0.50 and 0.50. The combined 2.25 first-half goals per match sits well above the divisional 1.26. The first-half result inherits the 1X2 problems on a shorter sample and is not readable.
Discipline (cards, fouls)Split — a genuine structural signal pointing into a lenient official
The team-level evidence is the clearest card story on this slate and it has a named mechanism. Liverpool’s PPDA has gone from 10.03 to 7.06, fouls from 10.08 to 13.00 and cards from 1.53 to 2.50 per match — the division’s cleanest side last season, now booking at 63% above its own rate, with the pressing figure to explain it. Bournemouth have gone from 2.37 to 3.25 cards on unchanged fouls. Both point up. The official points hard the other way: Oliver books 3.10 per match against a divisional 3.86, lets 7.4 fouls run per card against 5.60, and across his six previous matches involving these two clubs averaged 2.8 cards. A rising team signal meeting the most lenient whistle in the division is a genuine split, not a wash — the foul count is the part that is structurally established, the conversion of fouls into cards is the part the appointment argues against.
CornersDangerous
Both corner series have fallen towards the baseline and neither fall has a stated cause. Bournemouth 5.58 won last season to 4.50 now; Liverpool 6.11 to 4.25 — Liverpool’s drop of nearly two per match is the larger, and it sits alongside deep completions halving from 9.82 to 4.75, which is at least consistent, but four matches cannot separate a structural change from a run. Both clubs now read within half a corner of a divisional 4.68 at both ends. There is nothing to distinguish either side from the league average on this measure, and the one series that moved did so in a direction that the totals and BTTS readings do not require.

D · Early-season risk notes

  • Liverpool’s 2025–26 numbers were produced by a squad missing its three most recognisable components. Salah and Konaté were released and Robertson left free; £247m arrived, including a £123m winger and a £55m centre-back. The xG line looks continuous (1.77 to 1.74) and the personnel producing it is not.
  • The press change is real and measurable, and its consequences are four matches old. PPDA 10.03→7.06, fouls 10.08→13.00, cards 1.53→2.50, deep completions 9.82→4.75. Four separate series move together, which makes the change credible — but nothing here establishes what it does to results, and the result so far is three draws.
  • Bournemouth’s numbers look unchanged because the coach changed in the direction the style was already going. Rose’s PPDA (9.55) and possession (47.9%) are within noise of Iraola’s. That is a finding about two similar coaches, not evidence that a £54m rebuilt spine plays like the old one; the defensive personnel is almost entirely new and has four matches behind it.
  • The draw density is the dominant fact on this card and it is not a projection. Bournemouth 18 draws in 38 plus three in four; Liverpool 9 in 38 plus three in four; six of their eight matches this season level. It is reported as a counted rate, and it is the reason the result-based families are rated as they are.
  • Most sensitive: 1X2, Asian handicap and the clean-sheet sub-family — all three need a settled result distribution, and this pairing’s distribution is overwhelmingly concentrated on the draw. Least sensitive: BTTS and Totals — both rest on rates that held across 84 matches, two coaching changes and two complete squad rebuilds.

E · Short market-type guide

  • BTTS is more robust than 1X2 here because it is the one rate that survived both coaching changes at both clubs. 65.8% and 68.4% were the division’s two highest last season against a 56.1% line; Bournemouth are at 100% over four and have neither kept a clean sheet nor failed to score. The result records over the same period produced 21 draws in 42.
  • Totals is more robust than Corners because the goal series held and the corner series did not. Bournemouth’s xG moved 1.76→1.79 and Liverpool’s 1.77→1.74 across a change of coach; Liverpool’s corners fell 6.11→4.25 and Bournemouth’s 5.58→4.50, both to within half a corner of the divisional 4.68, with only four matches to tell a change from a run.
  • The fouls half of Discipline is more robust than the cards half, and the reason is the appointment. Liverpool’s 10.08→13.00 fouls has a measured cause in PPDA 10.03→7.06. Turning those fouls into bookings runs through Michael Oliver, who books 3.10 against a divisional 3.86, allows 7.4 fouls per card against 5.60, and gave 2.8 per match across six previous matches involving these clubs.
  • Any penalty-dependent line is the least robust thing on this card. Oliver awarded two penalties in twenty-nine Premier League matches last season, 0.07 per match against a divisional 0.24, and Bournemouth have had none at all this season. Whatever the box action, the official appointed to judge it has the lowest award rate on this slate.
  • The single fact that would most change which family reads best is whether Liverpool’s press holds up over ninety minutes against a side that has scored first in three of four. The 7.06 PPDA, the 13.00 fouls and the 4.75 deep completions are all four matches old and all point at a high-risk, high-reward structure; Bournemouth’s 1.25 first-half goals per match is the direct test of it. What would settle it is a fifth and sixth league match of pressing data, or a confirmed eleven showing whether Araújo or Jacquet partners van Dijk behind it — the lineup is normally published an hour before kickoff.
