A · Squad, promotion & transfer context

Neither club is promoted. Bournemouth finished sixth in 2025–26 on 57 points, and the way they got there is the single most unusual record on this card: 13 wins, 18 draws and 7 defeats, with 58 scored and 54 conceded for a goal difference of just +4. Eighteen draws is more than any other club in the division managed, and it produced a top-six finish on a goal difference barely above zero — a season of narrow, level matches rather than of dominance. Everton finished 13th on 49 points with 13 wins, 10 draws and 15 defeats, 47 scored and 50 conceded. The structural headline is that the home side has been comprehensively rebuilt and the away side has not. Bournemouth lost Andoni Iraola to Liverpool, appointed Marco Rose, and spent £54m against £0m of published income while replacing their centre-back, goalkeeper, right-back and centre-forward — and then arrived at matchweek two with four players out, one suspended and four doubtful, including two of the four signings. Everton kept David Moyes, spent about £84m on five players, and have won their opening two competitive fixtures by an aggregate of 6–0.

  • Bournemouth — turnover high, net spend −£54m, and every position the window touched is one that decides matches. Marco Rose, 49 and Leipzig-born, replaced Andoni Iraola on a three-year deal announced in April 2026 after Iraola’s departure was confirmed; Rose arrives having coached RB Salzburg, where he won three Austrian trophies, plus Borussia Mönchengladbach, Borussia Dortmund and RB Leipzig. The recruitment is unusually concentrated: Álvaro Rodríguez, a centre-forward, for £21.5m from Elche; António Silva, a centre-back, for £21.4m from Benfica; Juanlu Sánchez, a right-back, for £11.1m from Sevilla; and Michele Di Gregorio, a goalkeeper, on loan from Juventus. Total spend £54m, total income reported at £0m. The outgoings were all frees, loans or small deals — and one of them matters: Marcos Senesi, a centre-back, left on a free to Tottenham Hotspur, and Enes Ünal, a centre-forward, on a free to Getafe. Alex Paulsen (loan to Motherwell), Will Dennis (MK Dons) and Álex Jiménez (loan to Fiorentina) also left. So goalkeeper, centre-back, right-back and centre-forward have all changed hands in a single window, under a new head coach.
  • Everton — turnover medium-to-high, funded and directed, with the manager unchanged. David Moyes continues, and Everton are into a second season at the Hill Dickinson Stadium after what the reporting describes as a solid but unspectacular first year there. The window ran to five signings: Brennan Johnson for £22m from Crystal Palace; Merlin Röhl for around £21m from Freiburg, an attacking midfielder who spent last season on loan at Everton and therefore arrives already integrated; Tyrique George for £18m from Chelsea; Hayden Hackney for £16m from Middlesbrough; and Christian Nørgaard for £6.8m from Arsenal. One source gives the Röhl fee as around £22m rather than £21m; the difference is recorded rather than resolved. Out: Dwight McNeil for £22m to Crystal Palace, reported as part of the Johnson swap, and Idrissa Gueye to Al Diriyah, with Séamus Coleman, Tyler Onyango and Reece Welch released. No centre-back, goalkeeper or centre-forward signing surfaced — the money went to midfield and the attacking third, which is a club adding to a settled structure rather than rebuilding one.
  • Suspensions / absences. Heavily one-sided, and this is the fixture’s most concrete fact. Bournemouth have four out — Julián Araújo, Eli Kroupi, Amine Adli and Veljko Milošavljević — Ryan Christie suspended, and four doubts in Julio Soler, David Brooks, Álvaro Rodríguez and Juanlu Sánchez. Two of those doubts are summer signings, meaning half of Bournemouth’s £54m of recruitment may not be available for their first home match under the new coach. Antoine Semenyo, who scored 10 goals last season from 27 shots on target, remains the focal point of the attack. Everton have one absentee, Timothy Iroegbunam, and one doubt, Christian Nørgaard, with Kiernan Dewsbury-Hall and Iliman Ndiaye expected to retain their places.
  • Sample. One league match each plus a cup tie for Everton, and the two point in opposite directions. Bournemouth lost 2–1 at Manchester City in Rose’s first competitive match in charge, leading through Marcus Tavernier’s first-half goal before conceding twice in the final six minutes — a defeat that ended a club-record 18-match unbeaten league run. Everton beat Crystal Palace 2–0 on Merseyside on the opening weekend and then Preston North End 4–0 in the Carabao Cup on 26 August, with Thierno Barry scoring a hat-trick; Barry has four goals in two matches. Two fixtures is not a sample, and a narrow loss at the champions-elect tells you very little, but the availability contrast behind those results is real.
  • Referee. Chris Kavanagh, confirmed, with Dan Cook and Craig Taylor as assistants and John Brooks on VAR at Stockley Park. He is the highest-carding official appointed to any fixture on this card: 4.31 yellow cards per game across 29 matches in 2025–26, against 22.41 fouls per game, which works out at roughly 5.2 fouls per card — a low ratio and the signature of an official who books early rather than letting play run. His career line is 3.65 yellows and 0.09 reds per game across 1,176 yellows and 28 straight reds, with 81 penalties awarded in all competitions. One caveat on the appointment source: the article naming him headlines the fixture as Everton v Bournemouth, while the match previews and the odds file both have Bournemouth at home at the Vitality Stadium; the referee and the 29 August date match, the source’s home-and-away framing does not.

B · Style & early-season reality

Bournemouth’s 18 draws are not a curiosity, they are a description of how the team functioned: a side that pressed hard, made matches tight and finished level more often than any other club in England. Fifty-eight scored and 54 conceded across 38 matches is close to a goal and a half each way, and it produced 57 points because the draws were banked rather than converted. The interesting wrinkle is that the managerial change may be less of a rupture than it looks. Iraola’s Bournemouth was built on an aggressive, man-oriented press; Rose comes from the Red Bull school at Salzburg and Leipzig, where the same principles — high pressing, quick vertical transition — are the house method. So the coaching change points toward continuity of style with a different accent, which is unusual and mildly reassuring for anything that depends on playing pattern. What is not reassuring is the personnel: a new goalkeeper on loan, a new centre-back, a new right-back and a new centre-forward, with the right-back and centre-forward both doubtful, four more players out and Christie suspended. A press is a coordinated act, and this is the least coordinated a Bournemouth back line has been in some time.

Everton are the more legible side and the one with continuity where it counts. Moyes stays, the shape is known, and the summer added rather than replaced — five signings, all in midfield or the attacking third, into a squad whose defensive structure is untouched. Röhl is the detail that matters most for early-season reading: he spent last season on loan at the club, so his £21m permanent signing is not a new player learning a system but an existing one made permanent, which removes the usual integration lag. Everton’s 47 goals last season were modest, and the summer was aimed squarely at that — Johnson, George and Röhl are all attacking additions. Two matches have produced six goals and none conceded, with Barry scoring four, and while a 4–0 Carabao Cup win over Preston is worth very little, the 2–0 against Crystal Palace is a genuine Premier League clean sheet. Against a Bournemouth side missing nine players to injury, suspension or doubt, the away team’s advantage in this fixture is availability rather than ability.

D · Early-season risk notes

  • Eighteen draws is the number that most distorts Bournemouth’s sixth place. Fifty-seven points from a goal difference of +4 means the finishing position overstates the margin by which they beat anyone; their season was a run of level matches, and the club-record 18-game unbeaten league run that ended at Manchester City was built substantially on drawing rather than winning.
  • Bournemouth’s £54m has been spent on exactly the four positions the early season punishes — goalkeeper, centre-back, right-back, centre-forward — and it is not yet clear how much of it plays. Rodríguez and Sánchez are both doubts, Di Gregorio is a loan goalkeeper, and Senesi, the centre-back sold on a free to Tottenham, was part of the structure being replaced. The reported income of £0m against £54m out means none of this was funded by a sale that also removed a problem.
  • The managerial change is smaller than it appears and the personnel change is larger. Iraola and Rose both coach high-pressing, vertical football, so the method is broadly continuous — but a press depends on coordinated distances between defenders, and Bournemouth have four defenders or wide players out or doubtful plus a suspension. Style continuity does not survive personnel discontinuity in this specific market family.
  • Everton’s two clean sheets are worth less than they look and their integration position is worth more. A 4–0 against Preston North End in the Carabao Cup is not evidence about Premier League defending, and Barry’s hat-trick came in it. What is genuinely meaningful is that Röhl spent last season on loan at the club before his permanent move, so unlike almost every other significant signing on this card he arrives with the system already learned.
  • Most sensitive: corners and the outright — both need a settled Bournemouth XI and territorial pattern, and nine players are unavailable or doubtful two matches into a new coach’s tenure. Least sensitive: discipline, where a confirmed early-booking referee, two pressing sides and explicit per-team lines all point the same way regardless of who is fit.

E · Short market-type guide

  • Cards are more robust than corners here because Kavanagh’s 5.2 fouls per card is a property of the official and both coaching methods generate midfield fouls by design, whereas corner counts need a settled front line and full-back pairing that Bournemouth may not field.
  • The three-way handicap is more robust than the 1X2 because −1 at 3.85 against +1 at 1.72 already encodes the fact that Bournemouth rarely win by margins — 18 draws, +4 goal difference — while the outright asks a single price to absorb four injuries, a suspension and four doubts.
  • Totals are more robust than BTTS because the over case can be carried by the home defence alone — three new starters, two of them doubtful, behind a new goalkeeper on loan — while BTTS additionally needs Bournemouth to score without their £21.5m centre-forward against a defence that has not conceded in two matches.
  • Availability, not ability, is the real asymmetry, and it favours the outsider. Everton have one player out and one doubt; Bournemouth have nine unavailable or uncertain including half their summer recruitment. Any market that assumes home advantage translates into home control is pricing a team sheet that does not exist.
  • The book has priced the total at 1.83 against 1.90 and the double chance at 1.28 against 1.32 — two families at effective indifference — while taking a clear position on cards at 1.60 over. That combination is a bookmaker saying it cannot separate the sides or predict the goals, but is confident about the temperature of the match, which is exactly the ordering the evidence supports.
