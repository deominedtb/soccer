A · Squad, promotion & transfer context

Racing Santander are promoted, and not narrowly: they won LaLiga 2 outright with 82 points from 42 matches — 25 wins, 7 draws, 10 defeats, 90 scored, 61 conceded. Ninety goals is 2.14 per match against a second-division baseline of 2.63 per match for both sides combined, and they failed to score in only 9.5% of matches. They are back in the top flight after a long absence. Celta Vigo finished 6th in LaLiga on 54 points — 14 wins, 12 draws, 12 defeats, 53 scored, 48 conceded — and qualified for Europe. The structural headline is that the established side is the one in trouble: Celta have no win in six, three goals scored, and the lowest expected goals per match of any club on this board at 0.90, while the promoted champions sit three points above them having scored eleven. Neither club changed head coach. Both squads changed heavily around them, and Racing’s comparison season is not from this division at all.

  • Celta Vigo — turnover high, deliberately so, and weighted to departures; manager unchanged. Claudio Giráldez remains head coach in his third full season, having stayed despite offers elsewhere. The club’s stated plan for the window was ten to twelve departures against five or six arrivals, with the priorities named publicly as a defender, a winger and a striker. Out: Manuel Fernández Arroyo to Cádiz, Franco Cervi free, and four loans — Carlos Domínguez to Real Oviedo, Unai Núñez to Espanyol, Manu Sánchez and Hugo Sotelo to Levante. In: Altay Bayındır on loan from Manchester United, 7 August, and Abdoulaye Faye on loan from Bayer Leverkusen, 3 August — two loans rather than transfers. Javi Galán also arrived free from Osasuna, a move documented from the other side on this board’s Osasuna card at 14:00 today. The goalkeeping position was openly unsettled through the window, with Andrei Radu named as the likely first choice ahead of Iván Villar while the club pursued Bayındır. A club that planned to lose twice as many players as it gained, and filled the gaps with loans.

B · Style & early-season reality

Celta have stopped creating, and the collapse is in the chance data rather than only in the results. Expected goals have fallen from 1.37 to 0.90 per match — the lowest figure of any club carded on this slate — shots on target from 4.03 to 2.83, and goals from 1.39 to 0.50. They still have the ball: possession is up from 50.46% to 54.23%. What they no longer do is get into the box or press. PPDA has moved from 12.88 to 14.59 and, more tellingly, opponents’ PPDA against them from 12.91 to 15.18 — both sides press less, so this is a slower, flatter version of the same side. The one column that has improved sharply is the defensive one, and it is worth stating because it cuts against the league position: deep completions conceded have halved from 6.26 to 3.00 per match, the best figure on this board, and xG against has fallen from 1.48 to 1.41. Their away record is the evidence: 0.33 scored and 0.33 conceded across three. This is a side that has become much harder to score against and has almost stopped scoring — four draws in six, three of them by a single goal or none.

Racing are playing exactly as a promoted champion tends to: the same intent, applied at a level where it is punished. In LaLiga 2 they had 52.39% of the ball, took 14.21 shots against 13.02 faced, and won 5.71 corners against 4.79. In LaLiga they have 46.25%, take 10.17 shots against 17.33 faced, and win 4.50 corners against 10.17. The corner column is the single most extreme number anywhere on this board — 10.17 conceded per match, against a divisional 4.94 — and it is not one result: their per-match counts read 10, 8, 10, 9, 6 and 18, so even excluding the 2–7 at Barcelona they concede around 8.6 a game. Their pressing has intensified rather than dropped (PPDA 9.98, more aggressive than Celta’s 14.59) and the cost is in the same rows: 9.83 deep completions conceded per match and 2.29 expected goals against. At the other end they have kept scoring — 1.83 per match against 2.14 in the second tier, failing to score once in six — which is the part of the promotion record that has travelled. The evidence that this is the level rather than a bad start is six matches of shot, corner and deep-completion data all moving the same way, against a season whose xG nobody published.

D · Early-season risk notes

  • Racing’s entire 2025–26 record is from a different division and its xG column does not exist. The 82 points, 90 goals, 71.4% over-2.5 rate and 2.93 cards per match were produced in LaLiga 2, a division running at 2.63 goals and 5.31 cards per match — a higher card rate than LaLiga’s. No public source publishes second-division expected goals, so the measure that would normally survive the step up is missing, and nothing here estimates it from their goal count.
  • Celta’s league position and their defensive numbers tell opposite stories, and both are six matches old. Eighteenth with no win, but 3.00 deep completions conceded per match — the best figure on this board — and xG against down to 1.41 from 1.48. Four of their six matches were draws, three of those 0–0 or 1–1. The problem is entirely at the other end: 0.90 xG and 2.83 shots on target per match. Reading them as a bad team rather than a non-scoring one would misread every row above.
  • No absence explains Celta’s form. No source reached lists an unavailable Celta player. That removes the usual caveat and replaces it with a harder one: whatever has changed is structural or is noise, and six matches cannot separate them. Their window was deliberately ten-to-twelve out and five-to-six in, filled with two loans, so a squad-composition explanation is available but not demonstrated.
  • Racing’s corner and shot deficits include one result that distorts every average. The 2–7 at Barcelona on 16 September contributed 18 corners conceded and 30 shots faced. It cannot be excluded and should not be treated as typical; the per-match figures with and without it are given in the corners row above precisely so the difference is visible.
  • Most sensitive: the Asian handicap and 1X2 — the first because Celta have drawn four of six with a goal difference of −3, the second because the two clubs’ comparison seasons are in different divisions and the table has them the wrong way round. Least sensitive: corners — Racing’s 10.17 conceded repeats in every one of six matches and is corroborated by shots faced, deep completions, possession and PPDA at once, and Celta’s sub-divisional rate at both ends has held for two seasons.

E · Short market-type guide

  • Corners are more robust than the match result here because Racing’s 10.17 conceded per match repeats across all six fixtures and is explained by four independent columns — 17.33 shots faced, 9.83 deep completions conceded, 46.3% possession, PPDA 9.98 — while the result question sets a 54-point side that cannot score against a promoted champion whose last season has no xG at all.
  • Racing’s side of BTTS is more robust than Celta’s because their 83.3% both-scored rate continues a 73.8% rate from the division below and is backed by 2.29 xG against, whereas Celta’s 50% failed-to-score rate is a six-match figure against 15.8% across the whole of last season.
  • A first-half total is more robust than a first-half result because the two clubs sit at roughly half and double the divisional first-half rate respectively — Celta 0.33 for and 0.50 against, Racing 1.50 and 1.50 — while Celta’s four draws in six make any margin inside 45 minutes unreadable.
  • The penalty element of the discipline row is more robust than the card element because de Burgos has averaged 0.47 penalties per match across 19 matches and 0.67 across three this season, and awarded three in his two previous Celta matches — whereas his card rate (3.79, then 5.00) has moved sharply between the two seasons and the two clubs sit on opposite sides of the divisional foul rate.
  • The single fact that would most change which family I choose is whether Celta’s 0.90 expected goals is the squad or the sample. They planned twelve departures and made two loan signings, they have no absentees, and their shots on target have fallen from 4.03 to 2.83 — but six matches with four draws is exactly the size at which a scoring drought and a scoring problem look identical. What would settle it is a published per-90 breakdown of their chance creation with and without the players who left, or simply five more matches; neither exists today, and the totals row in particular rests on which reading is right.
