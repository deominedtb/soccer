The one thing shaping every game on this card

Four of the twelve clubs on this card are working under a head coach appointed this summer: Davide Ancelotti at Lille on 1 June, Pierre Sage at Crystal Palace on 15 June, Enzo Maresca at Manchester City on 29 June, and Iñigo Pérez at Villarreal in place of Marcelino. Two more have changed division entirely — Racing Santander are up as Segunda champions after fourteen years away, and Venezia are back at the first attempt as Serie B champions on a record 82 points. The single fixture with neither a new manager nor a promoted side, Bayern Munich against Stuttgart, is the Bundesliga’s opening night, so neither team has a league match to its name. There is no fixture on this slate where last season’s table describes both teams.

That is a different problem from the usual early-season one. The familiar complaint is that the samples are small; here the large samples exist and are attached to teams that no longer do. Manchester City’s runners-up finish was produced by Pep Guardiola with Rodri, Bernardo Silva, John Stones, Nathan Aké and Tijjani Reijnders, every one of whom has left — the club has since spent a record £116m on Elliot Anderson and £81.4m on Ayyoub Bouaddi, and Maresca said this week he still wants “one or two more captains” before the window shuts. Villarreal’s third place belongs to Marcelino. Lille’s third place belongs to Bruno Genesio, now at Marseille, above a squad that has turned over seven in and twelve out. Reading any of those finishes onto tonight’s team imports a manager who is not in the building.

One side on the slate is fully describable from its own recent football. Alavés kept Quique Sánchez Flores, who arrived in March and now has his first full pre-season with the group, and changed as few players as possible on purpose. Everywhere else the usable evidence is the kind that survives a regime change rather than the kind that resets with it — Villarreal’s sixteen consecutive matches without a clean sheet, which outlived Marcelino’s departure; Venezia’s eleven visits to San Siro without a win; Elche taking roughly 35 of their 43 points at home. Those are properties of a squad. A league position, on this particular card, is not.

Structural consequence. The families that need to know a team’s current playing pattern — outright results, corner totals, anything that turns on how high a side presses or how a block holds its shape — are the ones this card degrades most, because pattern is the first thing a new manager or a new division changes. The families that rest on properties which outlive a change of coach hold up better: both teams scoring, and discipline in the two fixtures where a per-team card line exists to check the asymmetry against. Where the book has priced those two within a few points of each other, it is generally saying the same thing about the evidence.

B · Style & early-season reality

Racing won a 22-team division by scoring, and by scoring through more than one route: 23 goals from Martín, 16 from Villalibre and 9 from Elorduy is a distributed attack rather than a single centre-forward carrying a promotion. B · Style & early-season reality

Bayern’s 122 goals are the most any side has scored in a Bundesliga season, and they were produced by a settled front line under a coach in his third year. That is a high-possession, high-territory team that turns sustained pressure into shot volume, and the Allianz Arena is where it compounds. Two things complicate importing it wholesale into matchday one. The first is that a record season is by definition an outlier, and the numbers that will be quoted about this team all weekend are the extreme end of what it has ever done, not its centre. The second is that the midfield behind the record is not intact: Musiala is out on a long-view plan rather than a short one, Gnabry is still recovering, and Palhinha is a player the club has actively been trying to move, which is not the profile of a settled defensive-midfield rotation. Saibari at €50m is a genuine addition, but he is an addition to the department Bayern were already best in, and a World Cup and a Super Cup are not a Bundesliga baseline for him.

Stuttgart are the interesting side here precisely because their summer ran against type. They scored 71 goals last season — second in the division — and rather than cashing in they spent about €55m deepening the squad: El Khannouss and Sauer widen the creative options, Pejcinović adds a centre-forward, and Prömel is a straight functional swap for Karazor in midfield. That is a side built to keep attacking, and Hoeneß’s continuity means the system is not the variable. What is the variable is behind all of it. Losing Nübel was planned for; losing his planned successor in the second week of pre-season was not, and it means Stuttgart open the season at the champions’ ground with a goalkeeper who was not the first or second choice for the role. The evidence that the new midfield and attack work together is a 4–0 against Hansa Rostock — a CV, not a match. The evidence about the goalkeeper is nothing at all, and there is no honest way to size it.

B · Style & early-season reality

Alavés are the most legible side on this card, and it is a deliberate achievement rather than an accident. A mid-season managerial appointment usually means a compromised following summer; instead the club backed Sánchez Flores with continuity, keeping the block and adding narrowly where it had lost something — Mikel Rodríguez for Guridi, Valentini into the back line, Miguel Rodríguez as the one attacking investment. The early evidence matches the intent: four scored and one conceded across a 3–0 against Getafe and a 1–1 with Rayo, which is the profile of a compact side that defends its box and takes what the game offers. The caveat is honest and simple: two matches, both against opponents from the same part of the table, and a coach who has never before had this group from the first day of pre-season.

Villarreal are the harder read, and the reason is not quality.
